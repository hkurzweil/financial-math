Find your starting salary

What is a salary, and what does "median" mean? A salary is the amount of money your employer agrees to pay you each year for your work. But before any of that money reaches you, taxes are taken out automatically — so the number you see in a job listing is NOT what you take home. We'll calculate your real take-home pay in Section 2.

The median salary for a job is the middle value — if you lined up every person doing that job from the lowest paid to the highest paid, the person in the exact middle is earning the median. Half of workers earn more, half earn less. This is a more realistic target than the average, which can be pulled up by a few very high earners. Always look for salary data specific to the city or region where you plan to live — pay varies dramatically by location.

What actually hits your bank account

Your paycheck is smaller than your salary — and that's normal When a job offer says "$50,000 per year," that is your gross pay — the total before anything is taken out. But on payday, your actual deposit is smaller. The government requires your employer to automatically withhold (hold back) taxes from every paycheck before you get it. What you actually receive is called your net pay or take-home pay. For most entry-level workers, take-home pay is roughly 70–80% of gross salary. The table below breaks down exactly where your money goes.
What do these taxes actually pay for? Federal income tax: Funds the national government — military, federal agencies, national parks, interstate highways, and social programs. Uses a "progressive" bracket system where higher earners pay a higher percentage on the portion above each threshold.

State income tax: Funds your state government — public schools, state roads, law enforcement, emergency services, state universities. 9 states charge $0 in state income tax.

FICA (Social Security + Medicare): 6.2% of your pay goes to Social Security — a federal program that provides income to retirees and people with disabilities. 1.45% goes to Medicare — health insurance for Americans over 65. You pay into these now; when you retire, you'll receive benefits from them.

The 50 / 30 / 20 rule — a useful guideline, not a law One popular budgeting framework suggests spending roughly 50% of take-home pay on needs (rent, utilities, food, insurance, transportation), 30% on wants (dining out, entertainment, hobbies), and saving at least 20%. This is a starting framework — not a rigid rule. If you live in a high-cost city, housing alone might eat 40% of income. If you have student loans or childcare, your "needs" category will be larger. Use these percentages to evaluate your budget and identify where adjustments might help.

Financial safety net
Emergency fund savings i
What is this? An emergency fund is a savings account you build over time and only touch when something truly unexpected happens — a car breakdown, an unexpected medical bill, a job loss, or a home repair. The goal is to save 3–6 months' worth of your total expenses. Without one, any surprise pushes you into debt or credit cards.

Why include it in the budget? Treat this like a bill you pay yourself every month. Even $50–$100/month adds up steadily. Once you've saved 3–6 months of expenses, you can redirect this money toward other savings goals like a home down payment.
Goal: 3–6 months of total expenses in a savings account
 $50 – $300+
Student loan payment i
What is this? If you borrowed money through federal or private student loans to pay for college, you are required to make monthly payments after graduation (typically after a 6-month grace period). Missing payments hurts your credit score and adds fees and interest. This is a non-negotiable expense if you have loans.

How to find your number: Log into StudentAid.gov with your FSA ID to see your federal loan balance and use the loan simulator to estimate monthly payments. Private loans are managed through your specific loan servicer. Enter $0 if you have no student loans.

✏️ Write below the screenshot
Click below the screenshot image and write a paragraph (4–6 sentences) that answers these questions. Do not just repeat the numbers — analyze what they mean:
  • How much money do you lose to taxes each month in total?
  • Which tax took the biggest chunk out of your paycheck — federal, state, or FICA?
  • Your take-home pay is what you actually live on. How does that number compare to the salary number you saw when you researched your career? Were you surprised by the difference?
  • If your state has no income tax, how much extra per month does that give you compared to a state that does? If it does have income tax, what does that cost you monthly?
📸 Step 3 — Take a screenshot
Scroll up on this page to the budget progress bar and the three summary boxes that show "Monthly income," "Total expenses," and "Left to save." Make sure all your budget line items are filled in. Take a screenshot that captures both the progress bar AND the three summary boxes together.
📋 Paste into your doc
Switch to your Google Doc. Click below your last paragraph, press Enter twice, and type this bold heading: Section 2: My Monthly Budget. Press Enter and paste the screenshot.
✏️ Write below the screenshot

  • What is your single biggest monthly expense? Does that surprise you?
  • What percentage of your take-home pay goes to housing (rent) alone? Is that more or less than the 50% guideline for all needs combined?
  • Was any category more expensive than you expected when you researched it?
  • Is your progress bar green, yellow, or red? What does that tell you about your budget?
  • If you had to cut $200 from this budget, which category would you cut and why? What would you have to give up?
📸 Step 4 — Take a screenshot
Scroll down to Section 4 — "Your savings snapshot." Take a screenshot of the three summary boxes ("Monthly take-home," "Total spending," "Monthly savings") AND the green, yellow, or red callout box below them that gives you feedback on your savings.
📋 Paste into your doc
Switch to your Google Doc. Click below your last paragraph, press Enter twice, type the bold heading Section 3: My Savings and Emergency Fund, press Enter, and paste the screenshot.
✏️ Write below the screenshot
Write a paragraph (4–6 sentences) about your savings situation:
  • How much are you saving per month? Is this enough to feel financially secure, or does it feel tight?
  • Your emergency fund goal is listed on this page. At your current savings rate, how many months would it take to fully build that emergency fund? (Hint: divide the goal by your monthly savings.)
  • What does having — or not having — an emergency fund mean for your day-to-day financial stress?
  • What is one thing you would do differently in this budget if you could change one decision (career, city, or a specific expense)?
✏️ Step 5 — Stage 1 closing reflection
In your Google Doc, press Enter twice after your last paragraph and type the bold heading Stage 1 Reflection. Then write a final short paragraph (3–4 sentences) about your biggest takeaway from Stage 1. This is not a summary — it is your honest reaction. Consider:
  • What did this stage teach you about money that you did not know before?
  • Does the life you planned feel achievable based on what you found, or do you need to rethink something?
  • What would you tell a friend who was about to graduate and start their first job about budgeting?
