Why a 10% raise and not more? In most professions, workers see meaningful salary growth in their first several years as they gain skills, experience, and responsibilities. A 10% increase over a few years is realistic and conservative — some fields see more, some less. As your income grows, the temptation is to immediately increase your spending to match. This is called lifestyle creep — and it's one of the most common reasons people with good incomes still feel financially stuck. Planning for retirement

What is retirement, and why do you need to plan for it now? Retirement is when you stop working — by choice or necessity — and need to live off savings you've accumulated over your working years. Social Security will provide some income, but most financial experts agree it will not be enough to maintain your lifestyle on its own. The earlier you start saving, the less you actually need to set aside each month — because of compound interest: your money earns returns, and then those returns earn returns. Time is the most powerful financial tool you have.

A 401(k) or 403(b) is a retirement savings account offered through most employers. Contributions come out of your paycheck before taxes, which lowers your taxable income now. Many employers also "match" a portion of what you contribute — that is free money. An IRA (Individual Retirement Account) is a retirement account you open yourself if your employer doesn't offer one. Both grow tax-advantaged — meaning you pay no taxes on the growth until you withdraw in retirement.
The magic of compound growth — a 7% average annual return When you invest in a diversified retirement account (like a target-date fund), the stock market has historically returned an average of about 7% per year after inflation over long periods of time. This means: every $100 you invest today becomes roughly $200 in 10 years, $400 in 20 years, and $800 in 30 years — without you adding a single extra dollar. This is why starting early matters so much. Saving $200/month starting at 25 produces dramatically more than saving $500/month starting at 40.

Will it be enough?

How much money do you actually need in retirement? Financial planners commonly suggest that retirees need roughly 65–80% of their pre-retirement monthly income to maintain their lifestyle. This is because some major expenses go away in retirement — your mortgage is often paid off, you're not commuting, childcare is long gone, and you're not contributing to retirement accounts anymore. We'll use 65% of your current Stage 3 monthly expenses as a reasonable estimate of what you'll need per month in retirement.

Then, using the "4% rule" — a widely used retirement planning guideline — we can estimate how large your total savings needs to be: if you can safely withdraw 4% of your savings per year without running out of money over a 25–30 year retirement, then your total savings target is roughly 25× your annual retirement spending.

What about Social Security? Social Security will provide some additional income in retirement on top of your personal savings — most workers can expect somewhere between $1,200 and $2,500 per month depending on their career earnings and when they claim benefits. This calculator does not include Social Security because the future of the program is uncertain and benefit amounts vary widely. Think of it as a bonus on top of your personal savings, not the foundation.
